Design & Setting

  • Design: Retrospective single-center cohort study
  • Country: China (Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University, Xi’an, Shaanxi)
  • Population: 1736 anti-CENP-B-positive patients (initially screened 2137; 1756 eligible after exclusions; final analytic n=1736)
  • Time frame: January 2016 – December 2023
  • Sex / Age: 93.84% female (1629/1736); median age 55 years (IQR 47–64); range 8–94 years

Inclusion / Exclusion Criteria (verbatim)

“Patients with anti-CENP-B positivity identified during the study period and sufficient clinical information for diagnostic classification and analysis were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included: a single outpatient visit with a pending diagnosis noted or solely non-specific symptoms (e.g. pain, abdominal pain). A total of 2137 anti-CENP-B-positive patients were initially screened, and 1756 were ultimately included in the analysis.” (Methods)

“In the present study, the term ‘ACA positivity’ was used operationally to refer to anti-CENP-B positivity detected by LIA. Anti-CENP-B reactivity was categorized as weak (+), moderate (++), or strong (+++), according to the semiquantitative band-intensity interpretation used by the clinical laboratory. For patients with repeated anti-CENP-B measurements, only the earliest eligible anti-CENP-B-positive result during the study period was selected as the index result…” (Methods)

Case / Control & Grouping

No formal case-control design. Four clinical groups defined:

  • Defined disease group (n=950, 54.72%): SLE, SS, SSc, RA, IIM, AAV, AOSD, SpA, Takayasu, Behcet, PsA, PBC, AIH, overlap syndromes
  • UCTD group (n=298, 17.17%)
  • Other systemic disease group (n=394, 22.70%)
  • Unclassified group (n=94, 5.41%)

Outcome / Variables / Statistics

  • Primary outcomes: Clinical disease spectrum, ANA patterns, coexisting autoantibodies, anti-CENP-B reactivity intensity
  • Key variables: Age, sex, anti-CENP-B band intensity (weak/moderate/strong), ANA-IIF patterns (centromere / mixed / non-centromere), isolated vs multiple autoantibodies, complications (cirrhosis, ILD, cytopenia)
  • Statistics: Shapiro–Wilk, Kruskal–Wallis + Dunn, χ² / Fisher exact, Bonferroni correction; GraphPad Prism 8.0.2; two-sided P<0.05

Purposes (Objectives)

“This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the clinical spectrum and serological profiles of ACA-positive (anti-CENP-B-positive) patients in a large cohort.” (Abstract)

“this study aimed to systematically characterize the disease spectrum, clinical features, ANA patterns, and coexisting autoantibody profiles of anti-CENP-B-positive individuals across different clinical states, with exploratory analyses of their associations with anti-CENP-B reactivity.” (Introduction)

Results (All Major Findings)

  • Defined disease group 54.72%; PBC 23.58% (224/950) and SS 22.32% (212/950) most common definite diseases; SSc only 6.11% (58/950); SLE 13.79%; overlap 16.63% (PBC-SS most frequent overlap).
  • Strong anti-CENP-B reactivity overall 77.88%; highest in Defined disease group (81.68%); AIH 91.53%, PBC 90.18%, SSc 89.66%, SS 87.74%, overlap 87.34%; weaker in SLE (66.41%) and RA (42.19%).
  • Centromere ANA pattern 57.37% overall; pure centromere highest in Unclassified (73.40%) and SSc (74.14%); SLE characterized by non-centromere patterns (49.62%).
  • Isolated anti-CENP-B 40.61% overall; lowest in Defined disease (29.26%), highest in Unclassified (60.64%).
  • Common co-antibodies: anti-Ro52, AMA-M2, anti-SSA, anti-nRNP/Sm. AMA-M2 enriched with strong reactivity (P=0.005); anti-nRNP/Sm with weak/moderate (P=0.009).
  • Complications: liver cirrhosis 10.19% (associated with strong reactivity, P<0.0003), ILD 9.33%, cytopenia 7.72%.
  • Among 77 UCTD patients with follow-up (median 68 months), 5 (6.49%) progressed (1 SLE, 3 PBC, 1 SSc); 4/5 had strong reactivity.
  • 17.11% of anti-CENP-B-positive patients lacked a compatible centromere ANA pattern.

Conclusion (verbatim essence)

“Anti-CENP-B-positive individuals represent a clinically heterogeneous population with PBC and SS as the predominant defined autoimmune diseases. Distinct ANA patterns, coexisting autoantibody profiles, and anti-CENP-B reactivity may provide complementary information for clinical phenotyping and diagnostic evaluation.” (Abstract)

“ACA-positive individuals comprise a highly heterogeneous population. Our cohort demonstrates that PBC and SS are among the most prevalent autoimmune disease associated with ACA… These findings expand the current understanding of the disease spectrum associated with anti-CENP-B positivity.” (Conclusion)

Innovation

Largest real-world Chinese cohort of anti-CENP-B-positive patients to date (n=1736); first systematic mapping of the full disease spectrum rather than single-disease focus; exploratory linkage of semi-quantitative reactivity intensity to phenotypes and complications.

Limitations (as stated by authors)

  • Semi-quantitative LIA only; no standardized titers or ACA isotype/subtype discrimination; no confirmatory assay.
  • Retrospective single-center design → selection bias.
  • Limited long-term follow-up for majority of patients.
  • No prediction model; small numbers in rare disease subtypes.
  • Serological discordance (anti-CENP-B+ without centromere pattern) not fully explained.

Critical Appraisal & Analysis (TASK 2)

1. Value (val) — Why this study was publishable in Clinical Rheumatology

“Large-scale epidemiological data on ACA positivity, especially within the Chinese population, remains scarce. Existing cohort studies on ACA, typically enrolling 50–200 cases, primarily focus on single diseases… thus lacking a systematic analysis across the full disease spectrum of ACA-associated conditions.” (Introduction)

Clinical Rheumatology prioritizes clinically oriented postgraduate-level rheumatology research. This manuscript delivers the largest real-world anti-CENP-B cohort reported from China (n=1736), shifts the traditional SSc-centric narrative, and supplies actionable serological phenotyping data (reactivity intensity + co-autoantibodies + ANA patterns). The findings directly inform differential diagnosis and organ-specific work-up algorithms that practicing rheumatologists and hepatologists use daily—aligning perfectly with the journal’s clinical accent and ILAR partnership.

2. What the study aimed to do (wtd)

“This study aimed to systematically characterize the disease spectrum, clinical features, ANA patterns, and coexisting autoantibody profiles of anti-CENP-B-positive individuals across different clinical states, with exploratory analyses of their associations with anti-CENP-B reactivity.” (Introduction)

The overarching objective was descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than causal: to map the full clinical and serological landscape of anti-CENP-B positivity beyond the classic CREST/SSc paradigm, and to explore whether semi-quantitative band intensity carries additional phenotypic information.

3. Summary of previous literature (spl)

“When tested by indirect immunofluorescence on Hep-2 cells, ACA produce a characteristic centromere pattern, which is a recognized serological hallmark of CREST syndrome variant of systemic sclerosis (SSc)… Beyond SSc, ACA have also been detected in other autoimmune diseases, including primary biliary cholangitis (PBC, 20–30%), primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS, 4–27%), and less frequently in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE, 2–11%) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).” (Introduction)

“Previous studies have associated ACA positivity with particular disease-specific phenotypes and outcomes, such as progression to definite SSc in clinically selected patients with very early SSc features and portal-hypertension-related complications in patients with established PBC.” (Introduction)

Prior literature established ACA as an SSc marker with milder phenotype, documented secondary associations with PBC and SS, and noted occasional presence in SLE/RA. Cohort sizes were modest (typically <200), mostly disease-specific, and almost exclusively non-Chinese. Intensity of reactivity and multi-autoantibody patterns received little systematic attention.

4. Critique of previous literature (cpl)

“Existing cohort studies on ACA, typically enrolling 50–200 cases, primarily focus on single diseases [2, 6, 11, 14], thus lacking a systematic analysis across the full disease spectrum of ACA-associated conditions. Furthermore, the clinical significance of ACA intensity and its correlation with immune characteristics remain poorly understood. Specifically, it is unclear what the clinical implications are for asymptomatic individuals testing positive for ACA.” (Introduction)

The literature was fragmented by disease silos, geographically narrow, under-powered for rare overlaps, and largely silent on the meaning of isolated or weak ACA positivity and on reactivity intensity as a continuous variable. No large Asian cohort existed that could address potential ethnic or healthcare-system differences in disease distribution.

5. Gap between the known and unknown (gap)

Known: ACA is linked to limited SSc, PBC, and SS; intensity may relate to SSc progression in selected cohorts. Unknown: (i) the relative frequency ranking of all ACA-associated diseases in a large unselected Chinese population; (ii) whether semi-quantitative anti-CENP-B intensity stratifies phenotypes across the entire spectrum; (iii) the serological and clinical fate of “unclassified” and UCTD anti-CENP-B-positive individuals; (iv) the clinical meaning of anti-CENP-B positivity without a classic centromere ANA pattern.

6. Rationale for filling the gap (rat)

“By providing a comprehensive description of this heterogeneous population, our findings may offer complementary information for clinical phenotyping and diagnostic evaluation.” (Introduction)

A large consecutive series from a major tertiary center that already performs standardized LIA + IIF panels can efficiently generate the missing panoramic view, generate testable hypotheses about reactivity intensity, and supply clinicians with local prevalence data needed for Bayesian interpretation of a positive anti-CENP-B result.

7. What they did in this study (wtdd) — Methodology

“This retrospective study analyzed patients’ data collected from Xijing Hospital from January, 2016 to December, 2023. ANAs were assessed using indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on Hep-2 and monkey liver tissue substrates… a panel of specific autoantibodies… were detected using line immunoassay (LIA… Anti-CENP-B reactivity was categorized as weak (+), moderate (++), or strong (+++ )… Patients were categorized into the following groups: Defined systemic rheumatic and autoimmune liver disease group… UCTD… Unclassified group… and Other defined systemic diseases.” (Methods)

Consecutive laboratory-identified anti-CENP-B-positive sera were linked to electronic records; diagnoses assigned by international criteria; earliest positive result used as index; statistical comparisons performed across four clinical strata and eight disease subgroups; exploratory reactivity analyses conducted. No prospective follow-up protocol or multivariable prediction modeling.

8. Results consistent with previous literature (rcl)

“Strong anti-CENP-B reactivity predominated in PBC (90.18%), SS (87.74%), autoimmune hepatitis (AIH, 91.53%), SSc (89.66%), and overlap syndrome (87.34%)…” (Results)

“ACA positivity may occasionally be detected in individuals without established connective tissue disease… Our findings corroborate and significantly expand the understanding of ACA-related disorders, affirming that ACA positivity extends beyond SSc to prominently include autoimmune liver diseases and exocrine gland diseases, notably PBC and SS.” (Discussion)

Consistent with earlier reports: high ACA prevalence in PBC and SS, milder SSc phenotype association, enrichment of AMA-M2 and anti-Ro52/SSA, female predominance, and occasional isolated positivity without definite disease.

9. Results contrary to / extending previous literature (rtc)

“Primary biliary cholangitis and Sjögren’s syndrome, not systemic sclerosis, are the most common definitive autoimmune diseases in the large anti-CENP-B-positive patient cohort.” (Key Points)

“SSc was only 6.16%… PBC (23.80%) and Sjögren’s syndrome (SS, 22.53%) being the most prevalent…” (Abstract)

The most striking extension is the inversion of expected disease hierarchy: in this unselected Chinese tertiary cohort, PBC and SS substantially outnumber SSc. Prior Western literature often portrayed SSc as the dominant ACA-associated disease because of specialty-referral bias. The high frequency of serological discordance (17% anti-CENP-B+ without centromere pattern) and the association of strong reactivity with cirrhosis also add novel dimensions.

10. Recommendations for future research according to the PDF (rfw)

“Future prospective, multi-center studies incorporating standardized serological assays and longitudinal follow-up are needed to validate these observations.” (Discussion)

“Further prospective studies are needed to determine the clinical significance of anti-CENP-B reactivity.” (Conclusion)

Authors explicitly call for multi-center prospective cohorts with quantitative titers, isotype analysis, longer follow-up of unclassified/UCTD patients, and formal prediction modeling of progression and organ complications.

11. Points of critique (poc) — Flaws & limitations

“First, anti-CENP-B reactivity was measured using a semi-quantitative line immunoassay, which did not permit standardized titer assessment or discrimination among ACA subtypes, and no serological confirmation was available… Second, the retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias… Third, the absence of long-term follow-up data for the majority of patients… Finally, no prediction model or predictive-performance analysis was performed…” (Discussion)

Additional critique: (i) diagnostic classification relied on chart review without independent adjudication panel; (ii) “Other systemic disease” group mixes unrelated conditions, diluting contrast; (iii) multiple testing across many subgroups without formal false-discovery control beyond Bonferroni for pairwise tests; (iv) no adjustment for concomitant immunosuppressive therapy that might alter autoantibody profiles.

12. Missed obvious points (mop)

The paper does not report: (a) quantitative CENP-B ELISA or addressable bead titers that could convert the semi-quantitative scale into continuous data; (b) IgG subclass or IgM ACA isotypes previously linked to SSc progression; (c) concurrent anti-CENP-A or anti-CENP-C testing; (d) detailed pulmonary-function or elastography data that would strengthen the cirrhosis/ILD associations; (e) ethnicity-specific allele frequencies (HLA) that might explain the PBC/SS predominance in this Chinese cohort.

13. Relevant points worth pursuing (rpp)

  • Longitudinal trajectory of the 94 “Unclassified” and 298 UCTD patients—especially those with strong reactivity and pure centromere pattern.
  • Mechanistic link between high-titer anti-CENP-B and hepatic fibrosis (complement activation? endothelial injury?).
  • Diagnostic performance of combined anti-CENP-B intensity + AMA-M2 + anti-Ro52 for early PBC-SS overlap.
  • Whether the 17% serological discordance represents assay artifact, early disease, or a distinct epitope subset.
  • External validation in non-Chinese populations to test generalizability of the PBC/SS predominance.

14. Order of reasoning in the discussion (oor)

The Discussion follows a classic inductive arc: (1) restate the historical SSc-centric view; (2) present the inverted hierarchy observed in the large cohort; (3) highlight serological heterogeneity and reactivity associations; (4) caution against over-interpreting intensity as prognostic; (5) translate findings into phenotype-directed clinical advice; (6) list limitations; (7) call for prospective multi-center work. The reasoning is transparent, data-driven, and appropriately conservative about causal claims.
